Context & Background

Dry Eye Disease (DED) is a multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterized by a loss of homeostasis of the tear film, accompanied by ocular symptoms, in which tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and neurosensory abnormalities play etiological roles. Its global prevalence varies widely but is estimated to affect 5-50% of adults, with increasing incidence in aging populations and those with significant screen time. Symptoms range from stinging, burning, and gritty sensations to blurred vision and photophobia, significantly impacting quality of life.

The therapeutic landscape for DED has evolved considerably over the past decades. Initial treatments focused on symptomatic relief with artificial tears. More advanced prescription therapies, such as topical cyclosporine (e.g., Restasis) and lifitegrast (e.g., Xiidra), target the underlying inflammation, offering long-term management for many patients. Recent innovations have also included steroid eye drops for acute flares, secretagogues, and biologic agents. The global dry eye market is a lucrative and rapidly growing segment, driven by an aging population, increased digital device usage, and a better understanding of the disease's pathophysiology.

South Korea has emerged as a significant hub for pharmaceutical and biotechnology innovation. The nation's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) maintains rigorous standards for drug approval, ensuring that new therapeutics meet high bars for safety, efficacy, and quality. The term 'homegrown' is particularly important here, as it signifies a domestic company successfully navigating the entire drug development pipeline, from discovery to commercialization, within Korea. This not only strengthens the national economy but also addresses local healthcare needs with products potentially optimized for the South Korean patient population, reflecting a growing trend of self-reliance and advanced pharmaceutical capabilities in Asia.
